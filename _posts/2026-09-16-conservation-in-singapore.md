## Loss of Reefs

Singapore has lost plenty of our reefs. Changi's humongous seagrass meadows, Pulau Tekong's large mangroves and even larger mudflats, Singapore has been reclaiming land at a steady rate. Once Singaporean researchers got to find out that our reefs are unique to Singapore due to its high resilience to climate change, we have already buried more than half of our reefs. Sadly, none of our reefs are resistant to being smothered by sand.

## Loss of Natural Forests

Singapore has been planning to develop natural forests for decades now. Some of these natural forests were originally slated for development when they were brownfield sites, but after 30 to 40 years, have regenerated into secondary forests.

## Environmental Impact Assessments

But as a City in Nature, we carry out our due diligence when developing a site. An [Environmental Impact Assessment](/articles/concepts/environmental-impact-assessment) would be carried out to study the environmental impacts of a development, to biotic and abiotic receptors. Sometimes however, these EIAs do not mitigate impacts due to weak enforcement due to lack of proper legislation.

## Nature "Versus" Conservation

But despite many situations where this seems the case, the idea of conservation and nature always being in direct conflict is false. Especially in the Singaporean context. There are plenty of examples of non-nature areas that need development. Towns that need revitalisation, brownfield sites, and soon HDBs that reach the end of their 99-year leases in 2050 and onwards.

Development is not always in contention with nature. But when development happens in a nature area, then does the conversation of trade-offs come into play.

Sometimes, development can enhance brownfield sites to turn them into young secondary forests. An example of the latter can be found in Admiralty Park. After it was gazetted into a park, the forest experienced human-aided regeneration.

## Reforestation

This place was once a sparse exotic-dominated secondary forest, dominated by the *Falcataria falcata*. After plenty of tree plantings and habitat enhancement, the new man-made secondary forest was native-dominated. But the process is very labour-intensive. Regular grass trimmings, regular root protection replacements, and lots of manpower.

However, reforestation is no joke, and the amount of money spent is immense. It has only been done a handful of times, and we're not very good at it. Plenty of times, the tree planting plot dies off or just stays small, serving no ecological purpose or succeeding into a Native-dominated Secondary Forest.

## New Nature

But not all green development projects create forests. Most rewilding projects by the National Parks' Board (NParks) create urban green spaces, which are no doubt inferior to forests in terms of ecological function.

### City in Nature, but how much Nature?

So many urban green spaces means Singapore is a City in Nature. But how much Nature do we truly need to retain for our ecosystems to stay functional? As of now, ecological connectivity is dwindling as new high rises are built in what used to be forests. Our biodiversity is also experiencing worsening climates. Housing needs are increasing due to new policies relaxing eligibility criteria, and thus nature spaces end up on the chopping block.

Would Singapore be content with a new City in Low-Function Nature? Or would Singapore choose to have a City in Singaporean Nature, where the plants in our green spaces are natural and unique to Singapore and can be a part of our national identity.

## What is the Criteria for "No Build"?

Sites which serve as important ecological stepping stones are also at risk of clearance. Even worse, sites which are **source** habitats (habitats that house a population) are slated for development. Tagore Forest is slated for residential development according to the Master Plan 2025, and houses half of Singapore's Raffles' Banded Langur population.

The Raffles' Banded Langur is classified as **critically endangered** globally by the [IUCN](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/39801/215090780). There are 200-250 mature individuals left on this earth, about which 50-70 reside in Singapore. This means that we have about 25-28% of the global population in Thomson Nature Park and Tagore Forest.

If removing half the home of a substantial global population of a critically endangered primate is still too weak of an argument to refrain from developing a forest, then what would be the threshold for a "no build" situation in Singapore? Do we have to threaten 100% of a global population of a species to finally say no?
