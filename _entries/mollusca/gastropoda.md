## Where seen?
You are almost certain to see a snail on almost every shore, even when the tide is not very low. Snails and slugs are found on moist rocks and boulders, on mangrove trees and other hard surfaces near the sea. They also creep among seagrasses, while small ones creep on seagrasses and seaweeds. Others plough through the sand. Yet more specialise on plants and animals of the coral rubble area or reefs.

## What are gastropods?
Gastropods belong to [Phylum Mollusca](../mollusca). They include shelled snails as well as slugs without shells. There are about 30,000 known species of gastropods, making them the largest group of molluscs. The class Gastropoda can be divided into three major subgroups: prosobranchs, opisthobranchs and pulmonates.

**Prosobranchia**
'Prosobranchia' means 'gill in front'. Most prosobranchs are snails with well-developed shells and breathe through gills. There are about 18,000 known species of prosobranchs, making them the largest gastropod subclass.

**Opisthobranchia**
'Opisthobranchia' means 'gill behind'. Most opisthobranchs are slug-like with a reduced shell or no shell at all. They include [nudibranchs](nudibranchia.htm), [sea hares](slugs/anaspidae/anaspidea.htm) and [sap-sucking slugs](slugs/sacoglossa/sacoglossa.htm). Opisthobranchs have an additional pair of tentacles called rhinophores, usually behind the first pair of tentacles. There are about 2,000 known species of opisthobranchs.

**Pulmonata**
'Pulmonata' means 'lung'. Pulmonates can breathe atmospheric air. The gills are reduced or lost and the mantle cavity works like a lung. Few pulmonates are marine. Most are found in freshwater or on land and include slugs and land snails. Marine pulmonates include some [limpets](gastropoda/limpets/limpet.htm) and the [onch slugs](slugs/onchidiidae/onchidiidae.htm). There are about 16,000 known species of pulmonates.

## Features
The snails most of us are familiar with typically have a large muscular foot supporting a visceral mass (the rest of the body and internal organs). In most snails, the entire body can be retracted into a protective shell. 'Gastropoda' means 'stomach foot'. Gastropods indeed have a large obvious foot. Most creep along on their foot, but [Conch snails](gastropoda/strombidae/strombidae.htm) can hop.

{% include img-grid.html items=page.galleries.gallery2 %}

**Shell facts:** Many molluscs produce a shell. A snail makes its own shell and stays in the same shell all its life. It does not moult its shell like a crab does. You can only remove a snail from its shell by killing it. Shells sold as souvenirs are mostly obtained by harvesting living snails and killing them.

A shell is made mostly of calcium carbonate, and shell material is added to both the outer edge and the existing shell so that a shell gets both bigger and thicker with age. The snail's shell is secreted by a thin, specialised tissue called the mantle. The outer surface of a shell is usually covered with a tough protein layer. Some snails may have a layer of fine brown hair, called the periostracum, on their shells. Pigment cells in the mantle create the beautiful colours and patterns of the shell.

**Mobile home:** The shell protects a snail from drying out as well as from predators. Shells come in a wide range of shapes, textures and sizes. These tell us a lot about the way of life of the owner. Some like [Nerite snails](gastropoda/neritidae/neritidae.htm) have nearly globular shells that help them stay on wave-washed rocks and make it difficult for crabs to grip them. Other rounded snails like [Moon snails](gastropoda/naticidae/naticidae.htm) burrow just beneath the sand. Some have spikes to keep off predators or large lips to protect them as they forage for food. Others have pointed tips to protect the siphon, a long tube-like body part. In some shells, the opening may be narrow and thickened so crabs cannot stick their fat pincers in.

**Snail door:** There's one problem with the shell: there is a big hole in it! This hole is where the snail comes out of its shell. Most snails close the shell opening with an operculum, a hard 'trapdoor' attached to the foot. The operculum may be thick and tough to prevent crabs from getting a grip on the edge of the trapdoor and digging out the snail. In others, the operculum is thin and flexible so that the snail can withdraw deep within the coiling shell out of reach of crab pincers. The operculum may also be used for more than just shutting the opening. In [Conch snails](gastropoda/strombidae/strombidae.htm), the operculum is shaped like a dagger and used like a pole-vault to hop along.

[Slugs](slug.htm) are gastropods that have lost their shells. Instead of shells, these animals have developed thickened skin or chemical and other defences.

**Other gastropod features:** Most gastropods have a head with a pair of tentacles. An eye is usually located at the base of each tentacle. Some slugs and even snails have eyes on the ends of long stalks. Snails also usually have a siphon, a tube created out of an extension of the mantle. The snail can stick the siphon out of the shell to suck water in and sample the water for chemicals, for example, to find food. In burrowing snails, the siphon is used to get water to breathe with. In some snails, the tip of the shell forms a notch through which the siphon emerges. This is called the siphonal canal.

In addition to a siphon, carnivorous snails usually also have a proboscis. This is an extendible tube which contains the radula, mouth and gullet. Some, like [Moon snails](gastropoda/naticidae/naticidae.htm), have a gland at the tip of the proboscis that secretes an acid to soften the victim's shell. With its radula, a hole is slowly drilled through the shell. The hole created by a moon snail is usually neat and bevelled.

**Gastropod babies:** Most snails (prosobranchs) have separate genders and generally practice internal fertilisation. Most slugs are simultaneous hermaphrodites though they may act as a male or female at any one time. When two slugs meet, they typically exchange sperm. Most gastropods lay eggs in a case, capsule or in gelatinous strings and masses.

Most marine gastropods undergo metamorphosis and their larvae look nothing like their adults. In some, free-swimming larvae with a tiny shell hatch out. Eventually, they settle down and develop into miniatures of their parents. In others, tiny crawling snails hatch out.

**Doing the Twist:** In their larval stages, gastropods undergo a process called torsion in their development from larva to adult. This process twists the body so that the anus moves directly over the head. Other body modifications usually ensure the gastropod does not dump wastes over its own head.

Scientists do not really agree on whether there is any advantage gained from torsion. Torsion is believed to be advantageous because it may help a snail carry its shell and retract head-first into its shell. The rearrangement also moves the mantle cavity and gills to the front and thus avoids these being clogged up by sediments stirred by movement. It also locates the osphradium (the sensory patch) to the front where it can be more effective in sensing the gastropod's surroundings.

All gastropods undergo some degree of torsion during development, and gastropods are the only creatures to do this. Some gastropods reverse the torsion later on in their development, such as slugs and nudibranchs.

**Role in the habitat:** Snails and slugs play their role in maintaining the natural balance as predators or grazers on seaweeds. They are in turn eaten by many other animals, including land creatures such as birds. Empty shells are vital to [hermit crabs](../crustacea/othercrust/anomura/hermit/hermit.htm). We should not take home empty shells from the shore because we might be depriving a hermit crab of a home. These shells eventually break down into calcium that baby snails need to make their new homes.

## Human uses
Gastropods are among our favourite seafood. These include [Gong-gong](gastropoda/strombidae/canarium.htm) and other [Conch](gastropoda/strombidae/strombidae.htm) snails, [Chut Chut](gastropoda/potamididae/obtusa.htm) and other creeper snails. Abalone is also a gastropod, not a bivalve. Wild populations of this snail are under severe pressure from over-collection. Many snails are also killed merely for their shells, for collectors or to be made into cheap souvenirs. [Cowries](gastropoda/cypraeidae/cypraeidae.htm) are among the most sought-after shells. Often, living snails are harvested and killed for the shell trade. The living snail is often more beautiful than its empty shell.

The study of marine snails has contributed greatly to our understanding of life, including human biology, as well as to medical and other applications. The study of cone snail toxins, for example, is leading to more effective pain relief. Marine snails can also be bio-indicators of pollution. The study of snail shells has also led to tough ceramics and other products.

## Status and threats
Sadly, many of our beautiful and fascinating gastropods are listed among the threatened animals of Singapore. Like other marine creatures, they are vulnerable to habitat loss due to reclamation or human activities along the coast that pollute the water. They are also vulnerable to trampling by careless visitors and over-collection for food and for their shells, which can affect local populations.
