## Where seen?
Molluscs are among the most familiar animals on Singapore's shores. They are found in mangroves, seagrass meadows, mudflats, rocky shores and coral reefs, and range from tiny snails to large clams and octopuses. Some groups are widespread and common, while others are hidden in crevices, under rocks or buried in sand.

## What are molluscs?
Molluscs are animals in the phylum Mollusca, and they include some of our most familiar and most unusual shore animals. The major groups on our shores include [Gastropoda](./gastropoda) (snails and slugs), [Bivalvia](./bivalvia) (clams and oysters), [Cephalopoda](./cephalopoda) (octopus, squids and cuttlefishes), and [Polyplacophora](./polyplacophora/chiton) (chitons). Not surprisingly, there is no single 'typical' mollusc.

Molluscs share a few common features, but they are also wonderfully diverse. They range from immobile oysters stuck to a rock, to slow-moving snails with large shells, to fast-moving squids with internal shells. While many are plain-looking, some such as nudibranchs are among the most colourful marine creatures. Octopuses are among the smartest invertebrates, while the giant squid is believed to be the largest invertebrate.

**Delicious!** Molluscs are among our favourite seafood. Some snails and clams have attractive shells, and many are collected for food or as curios. For a start, this group includes several of the most charismatic marine animals on our shores.

**Soft and squishy:** 'Mollis' means 'soft-bodied'. Among the few features shared by all molluscs is a soft body that is usually protected by a shell, a mantle or both. In slugs, the shell may be reduced or absent, and they depend on camouflage, toxins or other defences instead.

**Big foot:** In many molluscs, a large muscular foot makes up much of the body. The foot has cells that produce mucous to help the animal move over a surface. In some groups, such as octopuses and squids, this foot is highly modified for different lifestyles.

{% include img-grid.html items=page.galleries.gallery2 %}

**Terrible tongue:** Most molluscs have a radula, a ribbon-like structure covered with rows of teeth. It is usually used for feeding and can be modified to rasp, grate, grasp, cut, stab or inject poison. For example, drills are snails that use their radula to make holes through the shells of other animals.

**Other features:** Many molluscs also have tentacles, a pair of eyes and a pair of balance organs. Most also have an osphradium, a sensory patch inside the body that helps detect chemicals in the surrounding water. Most molluscs have gills in a mantle cavity, a circulatory system and a heart.

{% include img-grid.html items=page.galleries.gallery3 %}

**Sometimes confused with** [barnacles](../crustacea/othercrust/ciriipedia/cirripedia.htm), which are crustaceans rather than molluscs. Some shells found on rocks can look similar to those of limpets and barnacles but are very different in anatomy and lifestyle.

**Mollusc babies:** Most molluscs have separate genders, although many slugs are simultaneous hermaphrodites. The details of reproduction vary widely, and the young often develop from eggs laid in capsules, strings or other forms depending on the group.

## Human uses
Molluscs have been exploited by humans for millennia. Uses range from food and adornment to dyes, pearls, shellcraft and, historically, even money in the form of cowrie shells. In modern times, molluscs continue to provide food, while some species are being studied for medical applications.

## Status and threats
Sadly, many of our beautiful and fascinating molluscs are under pressure from habitat loss, pollution, trampling and over-collection. As with other marine creatures, the health of our shores is closely linked to the survival of these animals.
