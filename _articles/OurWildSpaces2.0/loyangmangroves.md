## Ecological Significance

There are multiple habitats within this site, consisting of a young secondary forest, mangroves and an intertidal mudflat with seagrass. This confluence of intertidal and terrestrial habitats is unique and rare in Singapore, where habitat types have mostly been isolated and fragmented due to development.

According to historical maps, the mangroves have been around since 1945, which is almost a century ago. The mangroves are also home to Bruguiera hainesii, a naturally occurring hybrid between two species, Bruguiera gymnorhiza and Bruguiera cylindrica. Natural hybrids form only through rare chance encounters over long periods of time, requiring highly stable long-term conditions. This supports the conclusion that the mangroves have become a mature ecosystem over many years.

![https://images.naturehungry.sg/photos/OWS2/Loyang-Mangroves-2.jpg](https://images.naturehungry.sg/photos/OWS2/Loyang-Mangroves-2.jpg)

With the proximity of this site to Ubin, it is plausible that the area serves as a refuge for wildlife in Ubin, especially if the wildlife population in Ubin has expanded. As a Greater Mousedeer was found stranded at the nearby Changi Beach Club in 2024, it would not be surprising if a population were to turn up here. It is also an indication that this site may serve as a landing point and dispersion pathway for wildlife currently restricted to Ubin including the mousedeer, and potentially the White-rumped Shama, allowing them to potentially establish populations in mainland Singapore.

## Why is This Area Threatened?

![https://images.naturehungry.sg/photos/OWS2/Loyang-Mangroves-3.jpg](https://images.naturehungry.sg/photos/OWS2/Loyang-Mangroves-3.jpg)

Most of the forested area bordering the mangrove is currently zoned for residential development, with a small strip along Sungei Loyang zoned for a future linear park.

## Our Hopes for the Site

Preserve the mangroves, a highly threatened habitat type of which only 0.5% of Singapore’s original extent remains. As much of the surrounding secondary forest as possible should also be conserved to maintain its role as a stepping stone for wildlife dispersing from Pulau Ubin. Strengthening planting between the site and Pasir Ris Park could further improve ecological connectivity.

![https://images.naturehungry.sg/photos/OWS2/Loyang-Mangroves-6.jpg](https://images.naturehungry.sg/photos/OWS2/Loyang-Mangroves-6.jpg)

The adjacent abandoned-land forest likely supplies the Loyang Mangroves with sediment, which is crucial for its long-term health. Development upstream could disrupt these flows and should therefore account for impacts on the mangrove system. As parts of the mangroves remain unassigned in the URA Master Plan, their long-term protection is also uncertain. We hope their conservation status is clarified and the wider forest-mangrove system is considered as a single, interconnected landscape.
