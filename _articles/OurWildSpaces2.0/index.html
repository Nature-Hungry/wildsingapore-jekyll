Our Wild Spaces 2.0 Report

{% include button.html href="https://docs.naturehungry.sg/Our_Wild_Spaces_2.0.pdf" label="View the OWS 2.0 Report here!" %}

As Singapore turns 61, we reflect on the value of our remaining green spaces in terms of biodiversity, climate, placemaking, and more. When our forests make way for development, we are often told that these values have been taken into account and that a fair trade between the benefits of development and conservation has been made. But how do we know what is a reasonable trade-off, if we do not even fully understand what we stand to lose before it is gone forever? This report features 10 remaining wild spaces that play an important role in safeguarding our remaining biodiversity, connecting our fractured forests, and buffering core nature areas. Crucially, their fates hang in balance, as current masterplans consign these spaces to other land uses. By revealing the unique histories, ecologies, and topologies of these sites, Our Wild Spaces 2.0 aims to equip readers with the ecological context needed to advocate and begin engagement for these spaces early in the planning process. We encourage Singaporeans to reconsider how we value our natural assets and ask what it truly means to be a ‘City in Nature’ : to recognise not only the myriad, tangible benefits nature provides, but also to value nature for its own sake.

How much nature should a city keep?

From forests to mangroves and coral reefs, proposals to transform natural habitats have prompted growing public discussion. What was once framed as a binary choice between development and conservation has evolved into a broader conversation about how to balance competing national priorities; it has become a more nuanced debate about what we value as a society, the role ecological evidence plays in informing planning decisions, and ultimately, how we decide which parts of our natural heritage are truly irreplaceable.

These questions are ultimately reflected in how we plan our city. Every line drawn on the URA Master Plan represents a judgement about how land in Singapore should be used. Some areas are prioritised for housing, others for industry, transport or recreation. For Singapore's remaining wild spaces, these choices can have lasting consequences - determining which forests are conserved, which are transformed, and which may eventually disappear.

The conversation surrounding Singapore's wild spaces has changed. Habitats once dismissed as "secondary forest" or "vacant land" are now recognised for their ecological value, supporting rich biodiversity, strengthening ecological connectivity, and continuing to recover decades after human disturbance. Rather than isolated pockets of greenery, they form part of a wider ecological network that allows wildlife to move around the island, supporting genetic diversity and contributing to the resilience of Singapore’s remaining natural habitats. This shift comes at a critical time. Since 1819, Singapore has lost 99% of its original forest, while its estimated cumulative extinction rate has reached 37%. In a country where so little natural habitat remains, every surviving patch carries even greater ecological importance. Despite these losses, Singapore's wild spaces continue to surprise us, challenging assumptions about what nature can sustain in a highly urbanised environment. The rediscovery of the Lined Blind Snake in 2019, after 172 years without a confirmed record, alongside the discovery of species new to science, such as Subaraj's Paddy Frog, reminds us that our remaining habitats continue to reveal ecological value in ways we are only beginning to understand.

Nature is not only persisting in spite of the city; when given the opportunity, it is recovering alongside it. Singapore has also committed itself to safeguarding this natural heritage. As a signatory to both the Glasgow Leaders' Declaration on Forests and Land Use (2021) and the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022), it has pledged to halt and reverse forest loss through effective conservation and restoration. These ambitions are reflected in the Singapore Green Plan 2030 through its City in Nature vision, while initiatives such as the National Parks Board's Ecological Profiling Exercise (EPE) have improved our understanding of how habitats across the island function as connected ecological networks. Discussions surrounding Maju Forest and Gillman Barracks in mid-2026 illustrate how our green ambitions are increasingly being tested in practice. Public discourse has moved towards broader questions of how ecological evidence should inform planning decisions, how alternative sites are evaluated, and what role biodiversity should play in shaping Singapore's future.

At the same time, these conversations have highlighted growing expectations for more meaningful public participation. Beyond consultation, many residents and members of the nature community are asking how their perspectives are represented in decision-making, whether engagements genuinely shape outcomes, and how the Government can foster a planning process that not only invites feedback, but is also seen to listen and respond to it.

However, conversations among the public often only begin after development proposals have already been announced. As such, opportunities to meaningfully influence planning decisions may be limited by then. As our understanding of biodiversity evolves, so should the frameworks through which the ecological value of forests is assessed and incorporated into planning decisions. The challenge is no longer simply recognising that nature has value, but ensuring that this value is consistently reflected when difficult trade-offs are made.

This document echoes that changing conversation. By identifying ecologically significant habitats at risk of development, we hope to encourage earlier and more informed discussions about how Singapore's natural heritage is managed. This report seeks to contribute to an ongoing dialogue on how ecological evidence, shared values, and the long-term stewardship of our natural spaces should weigh more heavily on the planning decisions that shape our City in Nature.

More than a report on conservation, this report invites Singaporeans to reflect on, and play an active role in advocating for the kind of Singapore we aspire to build. The choices we make today will shape not only the landscapes future generations inherit, but also their experiences, memories, and sense of place. As young Singaporeans ourselves, we believe these decisions extend beyond biodiversity or land use. They are about defining the kind of home we share, the values we choose to uphold, and ultimately, the kind of Singapore we hope future generations will be proud to call their own.
